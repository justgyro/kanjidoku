Sudoku that leaves you with a word.

The whole idea

Solve the puzzle. Keep the word.

Every row, every column and every box holds each character exactly once: the sudoku rule, unchanged. You don't need to know what a character means to solve a grid. A symbol is a symbol.

But two, three or four cells start shaded and empty. Fill the grid, read them in order, and they spell a real Japanese word built from the characters you have just spent twenty minutes with. The puzzle is the lock. The vocabulary is the reward.

Nobody learns a character by looking at it. You learn it by needing it, nine times, in nine different places, before the grid will close.

How a puzzle goes

First

Read the key

Every character in the grid is printed above it with its readings, its meaning and romaji. You never turn back a page to look something up.

Then

Close the grid

Pure logic, every time. No puzzle in any of these books needs a guess, and none needs a technique beyond naked and hidden singles.

Finally

Read the shaded cells

In numbered order, they spell a word. Write it in the space provided, along with its reading and its meaning. You can always check the solution in the back, so give it a guess if you are not sure.

The series

Four volumes, one ladder.

The series starts at the beginner JLPT N5 and N4 levels and builds up to N1, which covers the 500 commonest kanji at that level. So you learn kanji in order of difficulty, and within a level by how often they actually turn up. Considering that the top 1,000 kanji, much the same set as the 教育漢字 Japanese children learn in elementary school, cover over 90% of their general usage, you’ll cover a lot of ground.

Levels are drawn from KANJIDIC's frequency data, and each book says so in its front matter: the JLPT has published no official kanji list since 2010, so every list in circulation is somebody's reconstruction.

Inside

One puzzle to a page, and a proper back of the book.

The two complaints that come up again and again in reviews of kanji workbooks are that there is no index and no stroke-order guide. Every volume indexes every character it contains. Stroke order used to be squeezed into the practice squares; it now has a free document of its own, one per volume, where it can actually be read.

Why the grids can be trusted

Nothing here was left to chance.

Every grid is proved before it reaches a page. Three of those promises are the ones you actually feel while solving.

  • One solution, alwaysThere is exactly one way to fill each grid, and you can always reach it by logic. No puzzle in any volume needs a guess.
  • Every hidden word is realEach shaded-cell word is one Japanese actually uses, printed with the reading it actually takes, and so is every reading in every character key.
  • No compound twiceNo hidden word is used as a solution more than once in a volume. A hundred puzzles means a hundred different words to walk away with.

Made by someone who did the hours

The creator lived in Japan for twelve years and is fluent in the language. Which also means twelve years of knowing exactly how it feels: characters looked up countless times that still will not stick, a wall plastered with handwritten kanji index cards, the point where a textbook stops being a help and starts being furniture.

That is the whole reason these books work the way they do. A grid never asks you to memorise anything. It asks you to use a character until the puzzle closes, then hands you a word for your trouble.

It makes for a great supplement to your other learning materials. Which is why it sits alongside your textbook rather than competing with it, and why people finish one and start another.
